CHERRY NUTRITIONGROWER QUESTION 01 / SPRING 2026

Calcium in the leaf isn’t Calcium in the cherry.

Follow the water, and you can see why.

1ROOTS

Water and Calcium come in together.

2LEAVES

They pull most of the water, so most of the Calcium lands here.

3YOUNG FRUIT

Draws far less water while its cell walls are being built.

4SUGARS

Move on from leaf to fruit. Calcium largely stays behind.

So a leaf result tells you about the leaf. Whether the fruit gets its share comes down to water, roots and crop load as much as supply.

Draft captions

How do I know Calcium is reaching the fruit?

Andrew Smith grows cherries and is one of our agronomists. This is how he answers it.

“Once the flowers are off, a cherry is building its cells, and it doesn’t get any more after that. Calcium has to be there while it’s happening. A leaf test only tells me about the leaf. Calcium comes up with the water, and most of that water goes to the leaves, not the fruit.”

“So before I change anything I want to know what the block is doing. Crop load, water, roots, and a paired sap test read with the soil results. Measure first, understand second, intervene third.”

When the results call for it, we make a prescription blend for that block, with Power-Cal where a foliar Calcium fits.

Sources, wording and image notes

Whose words. Andrew’s lines are drafted from three places: his July 2026 Cherry Nutrition webinar (“cherries don’t get any more cells once they get past this cell division, we only really start to expand them”; Calcium as the foundation for cell wall strength early in the season), his Cherry Grower Webinar Development Guide (“Don’t start with the product. Start with the crop.” and “Measure first. Understand second. Intervene third.”) and the 2 October 2026 technical meeting (results that go “into the drawer”). The sentences in quotation marks are a draft in his voice, not a transcript. He rewords them before anything is posted.

The mechanism. Calcium moves with water and is poorly redistributed from older tissue; sugars move from leaf to fruit by a different route. Winkler, Fiedler & Knoche (sweet-cherry Calcium transport) and Washington State University’s sweet-cherry nutrition guidance support the limits of leaf Calcium as an indicator of fruit Calcium. Alkio et al. support early cell division as a contributor to fruit growth. Andrew’s guide adds that delivery to fruit becomes harder as development advances. Slide 2 is a cutaway built for this post over Josh Richardson’s orchard photograph (cropped to the sky and hill line, mirrored and thrown out of focus), with four close-ups: a root and its hairs in soil, the leaf blade, dividing fruit cells and the sugar route. It is an illustration and says so on the slide. It explains the route only; leaf counts, Ca markers and stream widths are not measurements.

What the post does not claim. No fixed cell-division window, uptake percentage, rate, or promise about size, firmness or pack-out. A leaf or sap result is not presented as a fruit measurement. The paired sap test wording follows the earlier claim guardrails: youngest fully developed and oldest still-healthy leaves, sampled consistently, read with crop stage and the block.

Product. The prescription blend comes first. Power-Cal is named once, conditionally, using its governed description: a liquid Calcium used while new roots, shoots and fruit tissue are forming, with foliar and watered-in options. No analysis or rate is given. Confirm fit for cherries at this stage with Andrew or Chelsea.

Images. Orchard photograph: Josh Richardson, north-east Victoria, September 2026, a block in flower. It gives crop context and is not evidence of a treatment response. Andrew’s portrait is the current staff photograph. The Power-Cal pack is the website product image. The tree, soil, roots, leaves and fruit in the cutaway, the close-ups, the crop-stage icons and the small leaf, fruit and soil pieces on slides 3 and 4 are painted in code for this post by a small renderer (stage_posts/calcium_render.py): each surface is geometry lit by one sun, with fixed seeds, so every run paints the same picture. The streams, Calcium markers and numbers are drawn over the top from the same geometry (stage_posts/calcium_art.py). They are made to look close to a photograph but they are illustrations, not photographs of a crop. Nothing on these slides is AI-generated; the September draft’s illustrated branch has been removed.
